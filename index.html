Order, not ornament

Between 1950 and 1970, designers in Basel and Zürich made a new style of typography. Today, many people call it Swiss typography or the International Typographic Style. Its posters, books and magazines have no ornament. The designers wanted information that is clear and objective. The reader must find each part quickly, and the layout must not hide it.

The style has three main parts:

  • A sans-serif typeface, for example Akzidenz-Grotesk, Univers or Helvetica, gives the text a neutral voice.
  • Text set flush left keeps the same space between all words. Its right edge is ragged.
  • A grid of columns and lines gives each part of the page a fixed position.

With a grid, all pages of a book or a magazine look like one family11 This page uses one grid too: twelve columns, and a line of 24 pixels.. The reader learns the layout on the first page and then finds each part at once. The designers did not want to show their personal style. They wanted to show the content.

Josef Müller-Brockmann:
the grid is an aid

Josef Müller-Brockmann (1914 to 1996) was a graphic designer in Zürich. In the 1950s he started to make concert posters for the Tonhalle in Zürich. On these posters, geometric shapes show the structure of the music. In 1958, he started the magazine Neue Grafik with Richard Paul Lohse, Hans Neuburg and Carlo Vivarelli.

Neue Grafik had text in German, English and French, in three columns side by side.

His book Grid Systems in Graphic Design (1981) shows how to make a grid. First, divide the page into columns. Then divide the columns into fields. The height of each field is a number of lines of text. Pictures and text fill one field or more. Thus each size on the page comes from the size of the text.

A grid does not make a good layout by itself. Müller-Brockmann wrote that the grid is an aid, not a guarantee. It gives the designer a structure, but the designer must still make decisions. A grid with too many columns gives too many possible layouts. A grid with too few columns gives too few.

The fields of the grid

A field is a number of columns wide and a number of lines deep. Here two pictures fill the seven columns on the left, and the text is in the five columns on the right.

Karl Gerstner:
a programme, not a solution

Designing Programmes

Karl Gerstner (1930 to 2017) was a designer and an artist in Basel. In 1959, he and Markus Kutter started an advertising agency, later known as GGK. For the magazine Capital (1962), Gerstner made a grid of 58 units. Between two columns are always two units. The same 58 units then give two, three, four, five or six columns.

This page uses the twelve-column version of his grid: columns of three units and gutters of two units. Every two, three, four or six of these columns make his six-, four-, three- and two-column versions. Only his five columns do not come from the twelve. The table shows each version.

In his book Designing Programmes (1964), Gerstner describes a method. The designer does not draw one solution. The designer writes rules, and the rules give many solutions.

Instead of solutions for problems, programmes for solutions.

This page uses the same method. Its rules are in a style sheet, and each distance comes from four values:

:root {
  --unit: 16px;
  --column: calc(var(--unit) * 3);
  --gutter: calc(var(--unit) * 2);
  --line: calc(var(--unit) * 1.5);
}

Emil Ruder:
the white of the page

Emil Ruder (1914 to 1970) taught typography at the school of design in Basel from 1942. For Ruder, the empty parts of a page are as important as the printed parts. The space inside a letter and around it has a shape too, the counter. A good layout gives these empty shapes a rhythm, as the rests do in music.

His book Typographie (1967) shows this with many examples. Ruder liked the typeface Univers by Adrian Frutiger, from 1957. Univers has 21 styles in one system of weights and widths. With them, a designer can make contrast in one typeface: light and bold, narrow and wide, small and large.

Ruder wrote that typography has one plain duty: to convey information in writing. Thus a beautiful page that is difficult to read fails. But he also asked for form. The reader sees a page before reading it, and a page with good proportions invites the reader to read.

Three posters after Müller-Brockmann

The three examples below follow posters by Josef Müller-Brockmann. Each example uses the grid of this page, in CSS, with no pictures.

The first example follows a concert poster from 1955. Its grid is the grid of the page, turned so that it goes one line down for each three units to the right. Each word, bar and rule starts at a crossing of the turned grid.

The second example follows his concert poster for Beethoven, also from 1955. Its rings are one line apart around one point, and the arcs end in steps.

The third example follows a concert poster from 1957 and uses all twelve columns of the page. The red areas are a number of columns wide and a number of lines deep, and some move down by whole lines. The names are on the red area, in the colour of the page.
